# Business strategies: what 14 best-selling authors share

Researched in November 2026 for owner-led councils. This is reference material: bring it in when a question is about reviewing the business, a hard stretch, cash, habits or a plan. It is never a member's voice and never the owner's facts; the owner's own record always comes first.

**How far to trust it.** No book was read in full. Everything comes from the authors' own free material (their sites, free chapters and articles), their interviews and talks, and publisher excerpts. "Holds" means the material read states it as advice or as the author's own practice, so a real count could be higher. Figures such as "six months of cash" are the authors' rules of thumb, not data. Quotes are short and were checked against the saved pages.

**The five that nearly all share (10 to 11 of 14):** find the one limiter and focus; track a few numbers on a schedule; keep a fixed review rhythm; face the facts and write the worst case; put cash first and keep a reserve.

My reading (impression, not data): the five are one loop seen from different sides. See the real picture, pick one thing, measure it, meet on it at a steady beat, and keep enough cash to survive being wrong.

## 2. Where they disagree

1. **First move when revenue falls.** Cut cost first and shrink to the smaller structure (Wickman; : one-month break-even budget). Or raise throughput from what you have and cut cost last (Goldratt; "never by cutting costs", a marketing line). Or reach out and redefine what you sell (Michalowicz). Or keep investing and cut only what cannot win: "You cannot save your way to winning the market" (Horowitz : he cut burn from 39 to 19.4 million a month while still investing, and cut payroll from 450 to under 100). Or make one cash-raising offer where demand is open now and cut nothing at first (Hormozi, a founder with 14 days of cash, third-party transcript of his show). Or cut hard but keep the core investment (Collins, Mulcahy). Or turn the whole team loose on a savings hunt with a number set first (a client firm in Harnish's telling: 40,000 euros a month needed, about half a million found,). My reading: they differ by cause, see "choose by cause" in section 4.
2. **Act now or wait for the data.** Do not delay (Horowitz on layoffs; EOS implementer : the businesses hurt most last time delayed critical actions). Versus wait two to four weeks for a trend before big moves and use the reserve to buy that time (Michalowicz). A customer in Goldratt's own interview made no changes for three or four weeks while he thought, "no panic" (Hoefsmit). Michalowicz and Wickman agree on acting at once on cash visibility and customers.
3. **How fast to let people go, or whether to.** Do not delay once decided (Horowitz), be ready for layoffs (Wickman), remove poor fits quickly and kindly (Dalio). Versus "maintain your people if at all possible" (: cuts are a last resort), voluntary shared pay cuts of about ten percent used only to avoid layoffs (Lencioni, his proposal and prediction), headcount down by attrition with no layoffs (Goldratt's Dow Corning case), reserves so deep nobody is cut (Southwest, Collins), and Harnish's own firm telling its team everyone keeps being paid, with a new loan as the backstop. A fan-site line attributed to Drucker says fast personnel decisions are likely wrong (second-hand, unchecked).
4. **How big a reserve.** Six months (Wickman), three months (Michalowicz; two to four weeks is enough to read the data), one month in the account plus six in a profit account (Miller), three months (a coach's client CEO, Harnish, second-hand), one year (one EMyth client's goal). Collins, Dalio, Hormozi and Horowitz give no figure. Wickman's implementer rule: pause discretionary spend if runway drops under four weeks (from an illustrative story).
5. **Planning horizon in a crisis.** Two weeks to two months, one-month budget refreshed weekly. A 13-week cash forecast every week (Wickman). Three to six months or six to twelve (Wickman); tiers from 10 to 50 percent less revenue (Wickman). The next hour, day or week (Michalowicz, crediting Dale Carnegie; Harnish: look ten years ahead but run tomorrow). Long compounding years (Collins, Clear, Hormozi's ten-year output).
6. **Debt and credit cards.** Hormozi uses a 30-day card float as his reasoning for customer-financed acquisition (his reasoning, not advice to copy). Michalowicz: no new debt to keep business as usual, avoid credit cards (he gives 17.3 percent average APR), debt grabs give "temporary relief but long-term agony". Wickman's implementers: max your lines of credit. Dalio, in his 2013 video: borrowing is good only when what it buys earns the repayment (his tractor against TV test).
7. **How heavy the rhythm should be.** Daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, yearly (Harnish, Lencioni, Miller, Wickman), about 20 percent of a leader's time by Lencioni's own figure. Against that: Hormozi keeps most days unbooked and keeps a light weekly test loop; Horowitz gives none; a fan-site line calls excess meetings a symptom (Drucker, second-hand). Wickman's Clarity Break is a solo option.
8. **Price in hard times.** Raise it when demand passes supply (Hormozi); raise it when you are not forced to and test on a subset; price beats volume (Harnish coach); a cheaper entry tier in a downturn (same coach). No price slashing (Michalowicz). A pre-sale bundle at a small discount, "six haircuts for the price of five". A premium "hard to refuse" offer (Goldratt, promotional). Hormozi himself differs on the first price: work free then charge or premium from the start. In his audiobook he says raise prices first, for real, before giving anything away free.
9. **Risk in a downturn.** "We're not taking risks" (Wickman, O'Donnell) and bullets before cannonballs (Collins), versus Holiday starting a bookstore in March 2020 (his account; he says the pandemic "nearly" killed it; no profit figures) and Hormozi saying risk appetite should grow with the business.
10. **Systemise everything or keep the human edge.** Gerber's whole method is systems, and Goldratt in his own words says services lack ready procedures: "the 'but' is quite big". Hormozi sorts programmes and mentoring as "education" and "expert-dependent", income but little enterprise value. Holiday says resist the pressure to scale. My reading, untested: mentoring that rests on personal judgement may resist full systemising.

## 3. One-page business-review checklist for the council

Use with the brief and facts file. If the brief has no number, mark the item UNCONFIRMED and ask, never guess. If the record is silent, write "nothing found". Authors in brackets are where the item comes from.

**Owner and head**
1. Where does the owner's week go: delivering, running, or thinking ahead? Would the business stop if the owner stopped for four weeks? [Gerber; Michalowicz; Miller]
2. What single thing limits the business now: leads, conversion, delivery hours, cash, or the owner? Name one. If it is cash: is it a cash-flow problem (cannot pay this month) or a solvency problem (worth less than owed)? Different cures. [Hormozi; Goldratt; Michalowicz; Dalio]
3. What is the worst case, in writing, and the plan B? Is any bet big enough to sink the business? What would we protect even in a squeeze, the reason customers choose us? [Hormozi; Wickman; Collins; Dalio; Horowitz]
4. Who outside the owner checks the numbers and hears the bad news? [Dalio; Collins; Horowitz]

**Money**
5. Cash on hand in months of operating cost, against 1, 3 and 6 months, and runway in weeks on the weekly sheet. [Wickman; Michalowicz; Miller]
6. Where does each payment go: tax, profit, owner pay, costs? Is profit a line or a leftover? [Michalowicz; Miller]
7. Margin per offer and per owner-hour: income less true variable cost. [Goldratt; Hormozi; Harnish]
8. Sale to cash: how many days, what is owed over 45 to 60 days, how much is paid up front? [Harnish; Wickman; Gerber; Hormozi,: 30-day gross profit above the cost to acquire a customer, and above twice that as his real-life minimum; gross profit already nets out delivery cost]
9. Customer economics: lifetime gross profit per customer against the cost to win one (at least 3 to 1); show rate, close rate, share paid up front; how many stay? Count the cost to win a customer in full (ad spend, pay, software, commissions), by channel, each month. [Hormozi;,]

**Offers**
10. Which offer earns most; would we start the weakest today? [Miller; Hormozi; Drucker]
11. Who is the one primary customer, and what do they say they value (asked, not guessed)? [Drucker; Miller]
12. Can a stranger say in one line what problem is solved and for whom? Result, likelihood, speed and effort for the buyer. [Miller; Hormozi]
13. Is demand above supply? If so, test a higher price; if not, the offer or the lead flow is the problem. For a stalled programme: is it a how problem (delivery, tracking), a what problem (the offer) or a why problem (fit with the owner's purpose)? [Hormozi; Goldratt; Clear]

**Growth**
14. Where do the next customers come from, in order: people who know you, referrals, content, strangers, ads? How many reach-outs a week? [Hormozi; Miller]
15. Weekly pipeline numbers (enquiries, calls, proposals, enrolments) against a steady floor and ceiling. [Wickman; Collins]
16. Is any big new programme tested small first? [Collins]
17. Do customers return, renew or refer? Is there a recurring part beside the one-off price? [Hormozi]

**Team (skip if none)**
18. Each helper: right person for the values, and right seat (gets it, wants it, capacity)? [Wickman; Harnish; Collins]
19. Does each helper know this quarter's top priority? Is there a regular one-to-one? [Harnish; Horowitz; Miller]
20. What could someone else do as well as the owner? [Drucker second-hand; Gerber; Holiday]

**Operations and rhythm**
21. Is the repeatable part written down (intake, onboarding, delivery, follow-up)? [Gerber; Michalowicz; Dalio]
22. Is there a rhythm that fits the size: weekly (numbers plus top three), quarterly (3 to 7 priorities), yearly (what went well, what did not, what next)? A solo option: a regular Clarity Break. [Wickman; Lencioni; Miller; Clear]
23. Is there one written goal for the next 6 to 12 months, with 4 to 6 defining objectives, or three numbers, a date and a reason? [Lencioni; Miller; Harnish]
24. Is there an issue list with severity and owner, solved at the root? [Dalio; Wickman]
25. Any decline signs: pride in past success, chasing more than can be done well, explaining away warning signs, lurching for a saviour, giving up? In a bad month, which trap: freeze, stampede (debt and card grabs), or aimless action? [Collins; Michalowicz]
26. Do the three assumptions behind the business (market, mission, what the owner is uniquely good at) still fit reality and each other? Check when things go well, not only when they go wrong. [Drucker]

Gaps the checklist cannot fill from these sources: a rhythm for a one-person business with a few helpers (EOS's Clarity Break and a weekly cash scorecard are the nearest), delivery capacity for mentoring, and pricing for recurring programmes.

## 4. One-page hard-times protocol: survive first, then thrive

Authors in brackets. Thresholds are the authors' rules of thumb. Steps 1 to 8 are survive; 9 to 13 are thrive.

**Survive first**
1. **Name it and steady yourself.** Do not freeze, do not stampede into debt or card grabs, do not drift into aimless action. Hold faith that you will prevail and face the worst facts. Write the decision on paper to take emotion out. A few deep breaths first: the leader sets the tone. [Michalowicz; Wickman; Collins; Horowitz; Dalio; Harnish]
2. **Get the real cash picture in a day, yourself.** Cash on hand, committed costs, what is owed to you, runway in weeks. Is it a cash-flow problem or a solvency problem? Update a short cash forecast every week. [Wickman; Gerber; Harnish; Dalio]
3. **Plan the worst case.** Short horizon: a one-month budget refreshed weekly, break-even as the target. Forecast cautiously: if the last two weeks fell 25 percent, plan for that to continue. Expect a 30 to 50 percent hit and confirm you survive a worse one. [Gerber; Wickman]
4. **Bring cash forward.** Collect what is owed. Ask for money up front, for example pre-selling next months' service. Only pre-sell what you can deliver. Pick one offer where demand is open now and run it as a short, clear push; to existing customers, a prepaid "buy more, get more free" capped at about 10 percent of the base, after budgeting the delivery cost. [Gerber; Hormozi (his words, his claims: in a refund crisis he sold his existing programmes by phone with cash up front and cut travel, not people); Harnish]
5. **Do not risk a hole below the waterline.** No big bet that could sink you; separate how likely a fall is from how bad it would be; no new debt to keep business as usual; if there is no reasonable hope of a better way out, do not plunge deeper, make a full stop. [Collins; Dalio; Michalowicz; Holiday]
6. **Cut to the structure that fits the smaller revenue, and decide what you will not cut.** Profit first: price, margin, dead costs. Do not expand. Stop failures and yesterday's breadwinner. Protect what makes customers choose you and the records you need (a customer history system). Ask the whole team where needless activity and cost sit, with a number set first. Where people costs must move, one idea is voluntary shared pay cuts agreed with staff. [Wickman; Michalowicz; Drucker; Goldratt; Horowitz; Lencioni; Harnish]
7. **Talk to customers this week.** Say you are open, ask what they need now, restate your services to match; serve rather than push; for a week or two, cut promotions and give with no expectation in return. [Michalowicz; Wickman; Drucker; Harnish,]
8. **Tell the team the truth, once and early.** If cuts are needed, decide once, do not delay, give a clear reason that the plan failed, and be visible. Where possible protect your people. Send something every day, facts only, bad news first with what you will do about it. Keep the meeting beat and meet more often. [Horowitz; Lencioni; Wickman; Gerber; Harnish]

**Then thrive**
9. **Find the cause, then fix the real problem.** Cash constraint, demand fall, offer problem, or a how, what or why failure? Use lead bullets, not a silver bullet. [Goldratt; Horowitz; Clear; Collins]
10. **Raise the floor.** Start a reserve with 1 percent of income weekly, aim for three months, then six; raise profit 1 percent a quarter; shorten the sale-to-cash cycle; run a cash-drivers session to find profit. [Michalowicz; Miller; Wickman; Harnish; Hormozi]
11. **Go back to basics and run the rhythm stricter, or reinvent around what you are uniquely able to do.** Lay groundwork for the rebound before it comes; do not hire early. Gain ground while others scramble. [Wickman; Michalowicz; Holiday own account]
12. **Strengthen the offer.** Raise the price when demand passes supply; add a recurring part; consider a shorter, higher-paid front end. [Hormozi, B; Goldratt promotional]
13. **Learn, then keep a steady pace.** Autopsy without blame; a yearly review; self-assess when things go well; a floor and ceiling you hit every month, not lurches. [Collins; Clear; Dalio; Drucker]

**Choose by cause (my reading, plausible, untested):** cash problem with orders waiting (Goldratt route: raise throughput, shorten the cash cycle); demand has fallen (EMyth and Wickman route: smaller structure, pre-sell, customer outreach); customers buy elsewhere (Horowitz route: the offer is the problem); a good business with one huge shock (Horowitz's fat-startup route: protect what wins, cut only what cannot).
